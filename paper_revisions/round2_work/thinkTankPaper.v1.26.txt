Abstract
Cognitive linguistics theorizes that language shapes human thought, and researchers have increasingly applied this principle to the study and development of large language models (LLMs). Several prior studies have investigated the metabolic costs of sentence formation using neuroimaging techniques such as positron emission tomography, functional magnetic resonance imaging, electroencephalography (EEG), and imagined speech reconstruction (ISR). To build on this work, we used EEG data corresponding to imagined English-language speech phonemes for ISR, combining it with an LLM trained on an abridged autobiography. The LLM-generated text responses guided the synthesis of EEG data from relevant phonemes, which we subsequently used to estimate corresponding metabolic activity and visually represent changes in simulated neurometabolic and electrical parameters. Notably, introducing pseudorandom microgap segments significantly altered simulated EEG-derived metabolic outputs (montage-mean CMRO?, Monte Carlo over microgap draws, R=2000; two of three tested sentences showed a 95% CI excluding zero, p < 0.01); a consistency analysis against real EEG from the same dataset found this effect's spatial pattern indistinguishable from real EEG, while fine time-frequency and source-space detail was not preserved by the composite-assembly procedure. This workflow is a text-to-EEG/metabolic simulation framework; it does not reconstruct unknown speech from EEG. Future work could expand the ISR system by incorporating lightweight or locally run LLMs, utilizing training data from larger and more diverse populations, and exploring truly random variability sources. Further optimization for broader hardware compatibility and implementation -- potentially including neural phantoms, emotional context integration, or human-computer interaction platforms -- may offer promising pathways for advancement. Overall, this work provides a preliminary foundation that may help guide the development of biologically inspired, modular, and adaptable ISR systems for research and practical applications.
Keywords: Imagined speech reconstruction; neurolinguistics; language production; large language model; cognitive linguistics; brain emulation


Introduction
Overview
Cognitive linguistics has increasingly theorized that language deeply shapes or interacts with thought (Price, 2012; Clercq et al., 2024; Imaezue, 2017)[1-3], inspiring the design of various large language models (LLMs). LLMs, however, require significant amounts of energy and computational resources to run, whereas the human brain performs far more complex computations while using remarkably little energy. Previous studies have explored the metabolic costs of sentence formation using neuroimaging techniques such as positron emission tomography (PET), electroencephalography (EEG), and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI). The use of imagined speech reconstruction (ISR) is one notable method for capturing these dynamics (Tang et al., 2024) [4]. Rothman et al. (2021) [5] experimentally validated a model of human brain metabolism, effectively estimating neural metabolism and converting between neuroimaging modalities. Because phonemes are fundamental components of spoken language, their production lies near the end of speech formation. While this study focuses specifically on speech and hearing, researchers recognize that comparable neurometabolic processes underlie other linguistic modalities, including reading, writing, and sign language. Given that EEG data from the imagined speech of English-language phonemes is publicly available, it can be efficiently encoded into a text string, nicknamed 'fractal' due to potential scalability. Using a self-similar (“fractal”-inspired) brain model, we developed software to convert any text string into a scalable-resolution, 3D model of neurometabolic activity based on ISR.  
Background
Cognitive Linguistics
The abstract ideal of consciousness itself cannot yet be reliably quantified due to ongoing debates regarding its precise definition and measurement, though discrete, correlated patterns of brain activity certainly can (Cogitate Consortium et al., 2025) [6]. Investigating the relationship between language and the brain—particularly the speech centers in general cognition—reveals the intricate neural landscape involved in linguistic processing. Researchers have long established the left hemisphere’s predominance in language function, especially within canonical regions such as Broca’s area, Wernicke’s area, and the angular gyrus (Price, 2012; Krishnan & Gandour, 2009) [1, 7]. Current evidence indicates that these regions are involved not only in spoken and signed language production, but also in semantic processing and multimodal comprehension. Together, this illustrates a distributed network architecture that remains critical to all human linguistic capabilities (Price, 2012; Clercq et al., 2024; Imaezue, 2017) [1-3].
Broca’s area, located in the left frontal lobe, primarily governs speech production and syntax while interacting continuously with the temporal and parietal lobes. The temporo-parietal junction drives auditory language perception and production, as functional imaging reveals increased activation during speech-related tasks compared with non-linguistic stimuli (Price, 2012; Krishnan & Gandour, 2009)[1. 7]. Notably, a meta-analysis revealed that both first and second languages recruit similar neural pathways, pointing toward the possibility that proficiency impacts processing without fundamentally altering the underlying brain regions involved (Sebastian et al., 2011) [8]. This finding suggests the potential robustness of the neural representations associated with linguistic tasks.  
Moreover, functional brain connectivity has emerged as a key research area, linking disparate regions across the left hemisphere during language tasks. For instance, studies utilizing fMRI have demonstrated synchronous activation patterns between Broca’s and Wernicke’s areas, thus highlighting a functionally interconnected network that appears essential for fluency and comprehension (Tripathi, 2024; Fedorenko et al., 2011) [9, 10]. The ongoing interaction between these areas supports the processing of phonological, syntactic, and semantic information, all of which researchers consider necessary for effective communication (Fedorenko & Thompson-Schill, 2014; Fedorenko et al., 2016; Friederici, 2004) [11-13].  
Recent advancements in the understanding of how bilingualism affects brain morphology indicate that individual language experience significantly shapes language processing, potentially resulting in adaptive neural structures (Burgaleta et al., 2016; Guan, 2024) [14, 15]. These structural changes appear to enhance linguistic capabilities while facilitating cognitive flexibility, underscoring the intertwined nature of language use and general cognitive functions.
Furthermore, empirical evidence demonstrates how language processing engages domain-general cognitive resources, suggesting that certain brain areas may function in both linguistic and non-linguistic tasks (Fedorenko et al., 2011; Heuven et al., 2008) [10, 16]. This overlap implies that while specific regions specialize in language, they also partake in broader cognitive operations, a dynamic that tends to blur the lines between language-specific and general cognitive functions.
In summary, language generation utilizes a sophisticated, highly interconnected neural framework in the brain that governs both speech production and comprehension (Fedorenko et al., 2011; Heuven et al., 2008; Fedorenko & Thompson-Schill, 2014). [10,11,16] This architecture not only facilitates language-specific processes but also contributes to a spectrum of cognitive functions, illustrating the profound complexity of human language processing, as mediated by underlying brain structures.
Brain Metabolism Measurements
Precisely measuring neural energy expenditure remains a challenging and often variable process (Saveliev et al., 2023) [17]. Prior work on scalable simulations of brain metabolic molecule concentrations provides significant insights, as researchers utilize advanced imaging and analysis techniques to explore metabolic variations across different brain regions. Ding et al. (2021) [18] constructed a comprehensive metabolome atlas, establishing a foundation for understanding metabolic differences across specific mouse brain regions, particularly during aging. Their findings indicate that these metabolic variations closely associate with functional and molecular changes, potentially serving as a reference for health assessments across various states, including disease.
Building upon this foundation, several studies employ imaging techniques to correlate metabolic activity with specific brain pathologies and neurophysiological states. For instance, Jacob et al. (2019) [19] used fluorodeoxyglucose to demonstrate significant decreases in metabolic activity within specific brain regions affected by binge drinking, illustrating the application of regional imaging in clarifying the functional consequences of external influences on metabolism. Similarly, Kleinridders et al. (2018) [20] utilized imaging mass spectrometry to elucidate regional differences in glucose metabolism across the brain, observing that metabolic responses in areas such as the thalamus and cortex are markedly distinct and emphasizing the necessity for region-specific considerations in metabolic research.  
Other research applies targeted metabolomics and imaging techniques to explore metabolic processes in relation to health states. For example, Zhou et al. (2022) [21] examined oxygen consumption across various brain regions, revealing that changes in neural activity correlate with altered metabolic demands and oxygen levels, which helps clarify how metabolism varies dynamically with activity levels. Furthermore, Képes et al. (2021) [22] tracked the effects of metabolic syndrome on brain glucose metabolism, linking regional metabolic activity with broader health implications.  
Technological advancements also enable high-resolution methodologies for mapping brain metabolism. Pang et al. (2020) [23] provided a detailed visualization of metabolite distributions across the mouse brain, enhancing the capacity to interpret metabolic data based on brain architecture. This work represents a critical step in integrating spatial resolution with metabolic profiling in simulations. Additionally, researchers have developed simulation frameworks like BrainScale to address the complexities associated with large-scale spiking neural networks, highlighting the importance of robust computational methodologies for advancing neuroscientific research (Wang et al., 2024) [24]. These frameworks facilitate the incorporation of metabolic models, allowing scientists to simulate brain metabolism in a scalable and efficient manner.  
In summation, the confluence of metabolomics, advanced imaging, and computational modeling establishes a robust framework for examining brain metabolic processes, showcasing diverse regional variations and their implications for understanding brain function in health and disease. Continued exploration in this field will likely prove essential for unraveling the complexities of brain metabolism and its role in cognitive and neurodegenerative disorders. 
EEG as a Metabolic Marker
Validating mathematical models to predict metabolic concentrations in the brain from EEG data integrates insights from neuroscience, machine learning, and clinical practice within an evolving field. EEG serves as a straightforward, non-invasive tool that researchers increasingly employ to assess brain metabolic states and estimate associated metabolic profiles.
Mounting evidence bridges EEG features with cerebral metabolic processes. For example, Westover et al. (2015) [25] introduced a method to estimate brain metabolic state using EEG burst suppression, demonstrating that electrophysiological features closely correlate with cerebral metabolic rates. This finding suggests researchers can leverage EEG data to evaluate specific metabolic conditions. Similarly, Schamberg et al. (2020) [26] developed a model representing transition dynamics among burst and suppression epochs, showing that underlying metabolic levels—quantified through ATP production—actively shape these epochs.
Furthermore, Crouzet et al. (2016) [27] contended that analyzing discrepancies between EEG signals and metabolic deficiencies following cardiac arrest refines predictive modeling for EEG recovery and neural function. This highlights the value of integrating EEG reactivity with metabolic assessments to improve outcomes in patients with neurological impairments. Recognizing how EEG features signal metabolic deficiencies enables clinicians to strengthen prognostication strategies and guide therapeutic decisions in critical care (Müller et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2019) [28, 29].
Machine learning approaches further enhance the capacity of EEG data to predict metabolic states. Dong et al. (2021) [30] introduced the Auto-Neo-EEG model, which uses machine learning to generate clinical EEG reports, thereby reinforcing the predictive precision of advanced computational techniques. Likewise, McConnell et al. (2024) [31] demonstrated that researchers can utilize digital biomarkers derived from EEG data to forecast various health metrics. In addition, combining EEG with modalities like functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) enriches models that predict brain metabolic states and vascular coupling (Pinti et al., 2021) [32]. This interdisciplinary approach emphasizes the utility of a multimodal perspective when modeling brain activity and metabolic functions.
Ultimately, ongoing developments in EEG methodologies, paired with robust mathematical and computational models, support more reliable predictions of brain metabolic concentrations. These advancements not only expand clinical monitoring capabilities but also offer transformative potential for interpreting brain health across diverse physiological and pathological states.
Brain Metabolism Models
Scalable simulations of brain metabolic molecule concentrations increasingly integrate neuroimaging techniques like EEG, fNIRS, and fMRI, providing researchers with a more comprehensive understanding of brain metabolism in health and disease. In particular, PET imaging—especially with 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose—plays a pivotal role in mapping regional glucose metabolism across various conditions. When investigating brain metabolic networks, researchers find that alterations in glucose uptake often indicate distinct pathological states, such as those observed in Parkinson’s disease (Tan et al., 2018; Li et al., 2020; Li, 2025; Matthews et al., 2018) [33-36].
The integration of functional neuroimaging reveals that metabolic abnormalities in regions like the putamen and globus pallidus frequently correlate with clinical symptoms in Parkinson’s patients, demonstrating how metabolic assessments can guide therapeutic strategies (Tan et al., 2018; Li et al., 2020) [33,34]. Furthermore, machine learning models successfully link brain metabolism to functional outcomes in cancer patients, suggesting that systemic health conditions may broadly influence cerebral metabolism (Yu et al., 2023) [37].
Experimental validation further reinforces these insights. For example, magnetic resonance spectroscopy enables researchers to monitor non-invasively metabolites such as N-acetylaspartic acid, GABA, and glutamate—molecules that frequently link to specific neurological functions and dysregulations in various diseases (Mao et al., 2024; Hyder & Rothman, 2017) [38,39]. Simultaneous imaging techniques, such as spatial frequency domain imaging, examine neurometabolic coupling, which remains critical for evaluating brain function, particularly during intervention studies (Wilson et al., 2019) [40].
EEG and fNIRS additionally detect changes in cerebral glucose metabolism during cognitive tasks, reinforcing the connection between neural activity and metabolic processes (Ghijsen et al., 2018; Crouzet et al., 2016) [27,41]. Advancements in imaging technology allow researchers to analyze real-time brain metabolism, contributing to a deeper comprehension of conditions like Alzheimer’s disease, where altered glucose metabolism appears to precede clinical symptoms (Camandola & Mattson, 2017; Goyal et al., 2023) [42, 43]. Moreover, novel methodologies like dynamic deuterium magnetic resonance spectroscopic imaging facilitate the concurrent measurement of multiple metabolic rates in vivo, offering a detailed view of brain energy dynamics (Tolomeo et al., 2018) [44].
Combining imaging modalities such as PET and MRI also transforms data acquisition by yielding comprehensive profiles of metabolic activity and structural integrity (Li, 2025; Radder et al., 2024) [36,45]. This fusion allows researchers to characterize brain pathologies more precisely and enhances targeted therapies by integrating functional, structural, and metabolic information within a single framework. Similarly, integration with advanced visual display software promises to expand current capabilities in simulating and interpreting complex brain metabolic processes.
Overall, advances in scalable simulations and their integration with neuroimaging techniques yield profound insights into brain metabolism, frequently correlating molecular pathways with observable physiological phenomena in both healthy and diseased states. Regarding the present study, simulating known metabolic pathways can enable researchers to estimate neural energy and processing costs directly, especially during sentence formation. Such a tool holds direct implications for neurolinguistics and ISR, although simulating active biological variability has historically presented challenges in prior work (Pliatsikas et al., 2016; Pliatsikas et al., 2021; Guan, 2024; Tang et al., 2024) [4,15,45,46]. 
Methods
Pipeline Design
In this study, we implemented neurolinguistic metabolistic simulations to generate metabolic predictions directly from text string inputs. The pipeline incorporates custom-built modules that integrate language modeling, phoneme-based electroencephalography (EEG) synthesis, computational metabolic modeling, and 3D brain renderings to model how cognition and physiology dynamically respond to speech-based stimuli. We trained the system using publicly available datasets and documents to establish a robust template for future development.

Figure 1 summarizes the overall pipeline. The process begins with a personalized GPT-4 conversational agent configured to simulate the style of an abridged autobiography (OpenAI, 2025) [47]. Next, the system phonetically decomposes the generated responses and maps them to an EEG dataset of phoneme-label recordings acquired from a public repository (LaRocco et al., 2023) [48]. A combination of neuroelectrical modeling (MNE-Python-based source localization) (Gramfort et al., 2013; Python Software Foundation, 2025) [49,50] and neurochemical modeling (gamma-band to CMRO2 translation) processes the synthesized EEG sequence (Pliatsikas et al., 2021) [46]. The pipeline then computes a set of user-selected neurovascular variables—including cerebral blood flow (CBF), pH, and partial pressures—and projects them onto a cortical surface via interpolation weighted by electrode proximity. Finally, the system produces animations to visualize electrical activation and metabolic changes across the brain during the GPT-generated response, while maintaining a dynamic user experience through the graphical user interface (GUI). Unless specifically noted, we applied default settings and displays for all included software to streamline deployment time and enhance accessibility. 
Conversational Language Generation
To begin the process of neuro-visualization modeling, a conversational agent based on GPT-4 was created using the LangChain framework (LangChain, San Francisco, California, United States). Multiple components were incorporated to enhance the conversational flow between the agent and the user, including the ability to return and retain context from previous messages with RunnableWithMessageHistory. These additions led to richer and more coherent responses in the style of the provided text.
User inputs were passed through a memory-aware retrieval chain backed by a Facebook AI Similarity Search vector database (Meta, Menlo Park, California, United States). The retrieval system populated the vector store with embeddings from OpenAI’s text-embedding-large model applied to pre-authored texts (OpenAI, 2025) [47]. Retrieved information found in the texts was fractioned and assessed through a combined_documents_chain to provide related context for modifying the agent’s output.
The GPT-4 model was guided by prompt engineering to mimic the thought process and linguistic style of the author’s introspection in the text. The prompt limited the agent’s responses to 20 words and instructed the agent to provide personalized messages in a curious and scientific tone. The overall system consisted of both a response generator and a question reformulator, allowing follow-up queries to be passed and interpreted as independent prompts without undesired characteristics. The final responses were formulated by the system and rendered on the GUI to be used as input for the phoneme-to-EEG phase of the pipeline.
Phoneme Decomposition
The response text was first cleaned by removing extraneous punctuation and white space at the word level. Each word was then passed onto a hierarchical phoneme extraction system that conducted a dual-layer lookup process. Specifically, each word was searched for in the Carnegie Mellon University Pronouncing Dictionary (CMUdict, Carnegie Mellon University, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, United States), which contains a corpus of over 100,000 words and their corresponding phonemic transcriptions in ARPAbet format [51]. To resolve cases where the word was not found due to capitalization or non-English origin, a neural grapheme-to-phoneme (g2p_en) model (Park & Kim, 2019) [52] in Anaconda 2024.10-1 with Python 3.12.7 (Anaconda, Inc., Austin, Texas, United States) was employed, generating inferred ARPAbet phoneme combinations based on raw text input.
The resulting phoneme sequences were then standardized. ARPAbet transcriptions tended to include stress markers (e.g., “AE1” or “AA2”) to portray emphasis on specific syllables [51]. Due to dataset limitations, the markers were stripped to produce a stress-invariant phoneme list. This design choice prioritized signal consistency over stress variation, given the nature and limitations of the available EEG dataset. The complete set of standardized phonemes was displayed to the user, employing an EEG segment retrieval process to assemble a synthetic yet biologically based neural representation of the original GPT-generated prompt. Users could also access voice recordings of the generated sentence output and the individual phonemes, which were synthesized using pyttsx3 (Bhatt, 2025) [52].
EEG Segment Assembly
Once the phonemes had been standardized, each element in the sequence was mapped to a corresponding segment from a phoneme-labeled EEG dataset collected from a participant under controlled laboratory conditions (LaRocco et al., 2023) [48]. The EEG recordings were acquired using a 16-channel configuration (Fp1-O2), with each phoneme corresponding to a 1-second segment sampled at 256 Hz. The recordings were used interchangeably to create temporally consistent phoneme-specific neural responses.
Each phoneme in the sequence was passed through the system, which retrieved the represented EEG file from a pre-indexed dataset using a numerical ID mapped to each ARPAbet unit (e.g., “B” ? 7 ? DLR_7_1.txt). The retrieved EEG file was subsequently parsed line-by-line to locate the first occurrence of the 0th index value, indicating that the first complete 256 samples aligned with the phoneme event. The first second of data (256 samples) was taken from the starting index and appended to an output buffer. A warning was logged if the required EEG file was missing or incomplete, and the corresponding phonemic segment was skipped in the final composed file.
To smoothen the discontinuity between words and maintain natural temporal structure, pseudorandomized “microgap” segments were inserted between words. The gaps were chosen by randomly sampling from a set of five additional EEG recordings within the same dataset and were randomly selected to be either 256 or 512 samples long, with an equal probability, introducing variability similar to natural pauses and spoken fluctuations seen in human speech. If added elsewhere, they could obscure the ISR process the work meant to simulate. With this addition to simulate sentence-level brain activity, EEG segments were concatenated into a continuous stream of voltage values across the aforementioned 16 channels, creating a synthetic EEG representation of the original GPT-generated text. 
Visualization in 3D
The visualization pipeline processes continuous EEG recordings from CSV files to generate 3D brain models through source localization (Gramfort et al., 2013) [49]. The modular architecture (EEGVisualizer) standardizes data ingestion, allowing seamless integration across processing scripts. Configuration parameters—including channel layouts, sampling frequencies, MRI options, and parallel processing cores—allow the framework to dynamically adapt to varying computational demands.
Preprocessing begins by parsing the raw CSV inputs, isolating numeric EEG measurements, and formatting them into standard matrix structures (EEG_Plotting). Metadata are encapsulated within an mne.info container to initialize the primary continuous data structure. To enhance signal fidelity and mitigate environmental noise, the reference is set to an average scheme, re-referencing signals across all electrodes at each time point. Spatial locations for the electrodes are mapped to the scalp using a standard International 10-20 montage configuration (Larocco et al., 2023) [48].
Source estimation requires establishing a anatomical forward model. A discrete source space is defined across the brain volume using an icosahedral subdivision (DistanceFunc) to balance spatial resolution with computational efficiency. A boundary element model is subsequently constructed to simulate head tissue conductivity profiles (calculate_neurovascular_variables). Combining the source space and conductivity model yields a forward solution that maps hypothetical electrical current dipoles to scalp-recorded potentials via a lead-field matrix.
To capture the statistical dependencies among electrode signals, noise covariance is estimated from the raw data using the Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage framework (Ledoit & Wolf, 2004; Engemann & Gramfort, 2015) [53, 54]. Because input recordings vary significantly in length, the pipeline dynamically selects optimal covariance estimators to maintain computational performance.
An inverse operator—integrating the forward solution, noise covariance, and sensor data—is then applied to estimate underlying cortical activity (plot_normalized_gamma_across_channels). Applying regularization and variance normalization to the inverse solution yields source time course (STC) estimates across discrete cortical locations over time. Finally, the framework renders an animated 3D brain model, projecting time-resolved electrical activity onto segmented left and right hemisphere surfaces based on the empirical EEG recordings. 
Visualizing Neurochemical Metabolism
We sent the synthesized EEG file into the first of two final phases of the pipeline, which produces an animated visualization of the neurochemical response across the cortical surface. The model simulated how phoneme-derived electrical activity reflected metabolic dynamics. Because brain activity is energetically demanding and oxygen drives energy production, electrical activity serves as a reliable proxy to estimate metabolic levels across different brain regions (Pliatsikas et al., 2021) [45,46].Similar to our previous study, the process began by performing spectral decomposition of the EEG signal using Welch’s method, as established in prior work (LaRocco et al., 2023) [48]. We segmented the inputted EEG data into overlapping windows to calculate the power spectral densities. This system prioritized the gamma frequency band (30-100 Hz), which is known to correlate strongly with metabolic demand. For each trial and electrode, we normalized the gamma-band power either globally or per trial, depending on the user’s choice. We then mapped these values to estimates of the cerebral metabolic rate of oxygen consumption (CMRO2) through a linear transformation, establishing 2.1 ìmol/g/min was considered the baseline metabolic rate (plot_normalized_gamma_across_channels). The derived metabolite metrics were not statistically independent, but dependent on the original CMRO2 estimates. 
Finally, we inputted the estimated (CMRO2) values into the relevant function (calculate_neurovascular_variables), which relies on a physiologically detailed model inspired by previous research on neurovascular coupling (Pliatsikas et al., 2021). Using standard values for hemoglobin affinity (P50 = 27 mmHg), buffering constants (BC = 26.0), and arterial pressures (pO2 = 100 mmHg, pCO2 = 40 mmHg), the following variables were calculated (Pliatsikas et al., 2021) [46]:
Cerebral blood flow (CBF) was calculated from the relationship CBF = CMRO2 / (O2 concentration × OEF).
Oxygen extraction fraction (OEF) was modulated using the coupling constant n = 2.28.
pCO2_V and pO2_cap—the venous and capillary gas pressures of carbon dioxide (CO2) and oxygen (O2)—were calculated via mass transfer and hemoglobin binding models.
pH_V—the venous pH—was estimated from lactate and CO2-related acid-base buffering.
ÄHCO2 and Älactate served as proxies for metabolic compensation and anaerobic flux.
Subsequently, the system generated outputs per electrode, per timestep, and per trial (if selected), which created a 4D matrix (variable × spatial × temporal × repetition) to help simulate dynamic cortical biochemistry.The EEG_Plotting module displayed the calculated data. The system placed the 16 physical electrode positions using Latin hypercube sampling (n =user-inputted number of virtual nodes, often arbitrary values exceeding 1000) for dense surface populations (Loh, 1996) [55]. Red dots represented actual electrode placements, whereas blue dots indicated interpolated locations placed throughout the brain model to simulate the rest of the cortex. The DistanceFunc module assigned a value to each point by computing the 4-nearest neighbor weighted averages based on Euclidean distances in the 3D hemispheric space.  Ultimately, users received a 3D animation in which the software rendered the selected metabolic variable frame-by-frame across 32 time slices, forming a temporal progression of the chemical values. The algorithm interpolated the chemical values across the cortical space through electrode-weighted proximity for each frame. A color scale displayed the surface, with ranges determined by biological markers observed in typical brain activity (e.g., CMRO2: 2.0–10.0, pH: 6.5–7.4, CBF: 20–90 ml/100 g/min). The software saved each animation as a .mp4 file and played it through the GUI, allowing users to reselect metabolic variables and refresh the phrase input created by the GPT agent.
Statistical Tests
We produced GPT-generated text with and without pseudorandom space insertion from the full pipeline, focusing microgaps on filling conTrials. We subsequently processed the trials to extract electrical and metabolic variables across 16 cortical channels per metric. We conducted paired statistical analyses across all neurovascular measurements using parametric and nonparametric methods, and we calculated effect sizes and residuals to determine the magnitude and significance of the observed variations. Visual outputs included GUI renderings throughout the pipeline process, metabolic and electric overlay maps, and regression collages that portrayed the transformation of phoneme-converted EEG activity to modeled cortical metabolism and electrical activity. These visualizations represented a single trial, illustrating the detailed spatial resolution of the outputs.
We performed the primary comparison between the GPT-generated texts with and without pseudorandom spacing across 3 trials. Acting as statistically related metrics and repeated measures, we averaged the values from each trial across the 16 cortical channels for each neurovascular metric (CBF, OEF, pH_V, pCO2_V, pO2_cap, CMRO2, ÄHCO2, and ÄLAC). We used paired t-tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests to determine the significance of the spaces, and we calculated Cohen's d to estimate the effect size. We computed residual means and standard deviations to quantify the consistency of differences across the channels. Considering the higher potential variability, we hypothesized that the differences with pseudorandom spacing would be significant. Without it, the ISR model could be excessively deterministic. Similarly, full consistency analysis across the three domains was performed: time-frequency (Hann-windowed periodogram shape and relative band power), source space (a validated, dependency-free spherical forward model with a depth-weighted, and average-referenced dSPM inverse. Further expanded in the repository, a leave-one-out real-vs-real baseline (n=44 source recordings) is computed so that synthetic composites can be judged against how similar real EEG segments are to each other, not against an arbitrary threshold.

Discussion
Interpretation
Integrating a pseudorandom microgap generator with the existing biological models significantly altered simulated metabolic outputs for two of the three tested sentences (95% CI, excluding zero; p < 0.001). Prior work in EEG-based ISR lacked the integration of LLMs and a broad range of biologically inspired variability (Pliatsikas et al., 2021; Guan, 2024; Tang et al., 2024; Buxton, 2024; DiNuzzo et al., 2024; Nelson et al., 2017) [4, 15, 46; 53, 54, 55, 56, 57, 58]. The added variability was deterministic, relying entirely on pre-recorded EEG data. Rather than serving as a tested experimental variable, the GPT model functions as an architectural component designed to structure and demonstrate the system's motivational layer. While the system can operate without it, the LLM frames the high-level linguistic abstraction. Furthermore, the self-similar architecture of the simulation allows the resolution of the visualization and parameters to scale depending on available computing power, spanning from mobile devices to supercomputers (Rae et al. 2024) [59]. System modularity enables researchers to modify any or all components to suit different experimental requirements. Simulating the metabolic costs of neurolinguistic processing represents one application, though speech-related pathologies and cognitive processes can also be simulated with minor adjustments (Pliatsikas et al., 2021; Tang et al., 2024; Buxton, 2024) [4, 46, 56, 57, 58]. Ultimately, this integration of an LLM and biological variance models the sentence formation process, progressing from higher levels of abstraction down to molecular-level insights.  
Limitations
The current model possesses several limitations. First, a single LLM model handled most of the processing, creating a reliance on online access and intensive hardware requirements, despite the model remaining non-essential to the core biological mechanics. Second, data from a single individual from a healthy controls study restricted the investigation of broader speech or neural pathologies (LaRocco et al., 2023) [48, 59]. Third, the model relied on pseudorandom rather than truly random variability due to the structural constraints of the prior dataset (LaRocco et al., 2023) [48]. Utilizing truly random variability would introduce unpredictable stochastic noise; while this might better capture natural biological variance, pseudorandom generation ensures deterministic reproducibility across trials. If gaps were truly random, the interpretation would shift from controlled state transitions to stochastic uncertainty, potentially obscuring baseline comparisons. Fourth, reliance on Python introduced portability and scalability constraints across specialized hardware architectures, although it enabled rapid initial deployment. Finally, identical statistical output values (such as repeated t-statistics and effect sizes across distinct metrics within trials) occurred because the underlying deterministic baseline metrics shared scaled transformations derived from the same source EEG segments. Despite these limitations, the proposed system provides a robust template for future development. The statistics above treat microgap realisations, not electrode channels, as the unit of replication: the 16 channels of a single synthesized composite are spatially correlated repeated measures, not independent biological replicates, and the eight reported neurovascular variables are mathematically derived from one underlying CMRO? signal (effective number of independent variables ?1 of 8) rather than independent lines of evidence.
Future Work
TThe proposed ISR system requires further development. Researchers can adapt the GPT model to run locally with fewer computational resources, or omit it entirely. Incorporating a large population of individuals, including those with diverse writing styles and languages, will enable the modeling of a wider range of linguistic capabilities. Adding emotional context and syntax to event responses may also improve naturalistic responses. Integrating a broader range of physiological data—including data from both healthy individuals and those with neuropathologies, augmented with genuinely random data—will enhance generalizability. Future work should validate the simulation framework's metabolic and electrophysiological outputs against an independent biological reference—continuous imagined-speech EEG or naturalistic pause data—since the present consistency analysis compares only against the same phoneme-recording dataset the composites are built from. The system could incorporate genuinely random (rather than pseudorandom, seed-deterministic) variability sources where appropriate, alongside data from larger and more diverse populations. Researchers can also integrate other neuroimaging modalities. Substantial software optimization will allow the system to run on a broader range of devices and networks, including edge computing environments. Furthermore, developers can extend the architecture to hardware utilizing neural phantoms (LaRocco et al., 2024) [60] and adapt it for diverse hardware applications such as human-computer interaction, bioreactor management, and AI agents.
Conclusions
Despite certain limitations—such as relying on a single LLM, depending on online access, using data from a single individual, and facing hardware constraints—the presented system establishes a robust template for future research and development. This study demonstrated that integrating a pseudorandom microgap generator and LLM into a biologically inspired, text-to-EEG/metabolic simulation framework produces significantly altered simulated outputs compared with a deterministic baseline, for the tested sentences; a consistency analysis against real EEG from the same dataset found the resulting metabolic spatial pattern indistinguishable from real EEG, while finer time-frequency and source-space structure was not preserved. We frame this as a demonstrated property of the simulation pipeline rather than evidence of improved biological realism in general, pending independent validation. By introducing scalable, fractal-like variability and modular components, the system supports a wide range of applications—from simulating neurolinguistic metabolic costs to modeling speech pathologies and cognitive processes. Researchers can adjust the resolution and parameters according to available computational resources, which further enhances the system’s versatility and suits it for deployment on devices ranging from mobile phones to supercomputers. Looking forward, developers can expand the ISR system by incorporating locally run or lighter-weight language models, integrating data from more diverse populations, and employing genuinely random variability. Optimizing the software for broader hardware compatibility and exploring hardware implementations—such as neural phantoms, emotional context, or human-computer interaction platforms—represent promising avenues for further development. Overall, this work lays the groundwork for a new generation of biologically inspired, modular, and highly adaptable ISR systems for research and practical applications.
